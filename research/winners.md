# Hutter Prize: all eleven awarded submissions

As of 22 November 2026, the [organizer’s ranking, “Current and Previous Records”](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/#prev), lists **four enwik8 awards and seven enwik9 awards**. The latest is Vladimir Ivanov’s **fx2-cmix-T: 100,424,672 bytes**, not its 96,996,198-byte self-extracting archive alone. Dates below are the dates printed in the ranking, not necessarily announcement dates. For example, [committee member Jim Bowery’s award publication](https://scanalyst.fourmilab.ch/t/hutter-prize-awards-largest-advance-in-20-years-on-its-superintelligence-benchmark/6468) announces the last three together on 30 September 2026, in the order shown below.

## What was being minimized?

The original €50,000 contest used **enwik8**, the first 100,000,000 bytes of the Wikipedia extract. It charged the archive **and the decompressor**, not a separate compressor. The €500,000 contest launched in 2020 uses **enwik9**, the first 1,000,000,000 bytes. In the standard self-extracting submission the score is `S1 + S2`: the compressor package plus the executable archive that restores enwik9 without outside input. The decoder is already inside S2; do not add it a third time. Program size matters: a trained model can shrink the payload but still lose once its weights are charged. There are also single-core time, memory and disk constraints; an unrestricted compression record is not automatically a prize record. See the [rules and alternative packaging](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/hrules.htm), the [organizer’s FAQ](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/hfaq.htm) and [Mahoney’s published benchmark rationale](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/rationale.html).

## Original contest: enwik8

All four awards went to **Alexander Rhatushnyak**. Each split below is published directly in the [organizer’s detailed contestant table](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/#contestants); gains and awards are from the [ranking](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/#prev). The starting record was Matt Mahoney’s `paq8f -7`, **18,324,887** bytes (18,289,559 + 35,328), a pre-prize baseline, not a fifth win.

| 1 | 25 Sep 2006 | paq8hp5 -7 | 16,898,402 | 174,616 | 17,073,018 | 6.8% | €3,416 |
| 2 | 14 May 2007 | paq8hp12 -7 | 16,381,959 | 99,696 | 16,481,655 | 3.5% | €1,732 |
| 3 | 23 May 2009 | decomp8 | 15,932,968 | 16,720 | 15,949,688 | 3.2% | €1,614 |

**1–2. paq8hp5 and paq8hp12.** These forked Mahoney’s PAQ context mixer: predictors estimate the next bit using different views of preceding data, and their estimates are combined rather than choosing one fixed context. A dictionary transform based on Przemysław Skibiński’s XML-WRT replaced words with codes before modeling. The hp series grouped codes for related words and common suffixes; hp5 tuned dictionary size specifically for enwik8. The embedded dictionary was not a free external vocabulary. The hp12 result improved *both* sides of the ledger: a smaller archive and a decoder of only 99,696 bytes. Mahoney distinguishes the winning `-7` build from `paq8hp12any`, a later source-available variant that makes the same archive at `-7`. See the [version history and source mirrors](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1323) and the [organizer’s submission history](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/#contestants).

**3. decomp8.** Match artifacts by date and total, not just by name: the organizer calls the winner `decomp8`; Mahoney’s [benchmark and archive index](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1440) calls the final May update `decmprs8` and lists earlier `decomp8` and `decomp8b` separately. He observed the earlier Windows decoder creating a temporary dictionary similar to paq8hp12’s. The final package is documented as a decoder plus data, not a released encoder; that observation supports dictionary use in the series, not a reconstruction of the final predictor or an attribution of the May gain to a particular change.

**4. phda9.** The [FAQ’s source-history note](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/hfaq.htm#getsource) identifies PAQ8 ancestry and separately released enwik-specific transforms; it explicitly says the full winner source was not released or required in 2017. Mahoney identifies the [original submission](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1165) as the Linux decoder and compressed enwik8, using a 176 MB scratch file. Later public builds change packaging, including where dictionary bytes reside. Neither their component sizes nor the later enwik9 result should be substituted for the 2017 award.

## Expanded contest: enwik9

The [official ranking](https://www.hutter1.net/prize/#prev) starts from Rhatushnyak’s **phda9v1.8, 116,673,681 bytes**, dated 4 July 2019 and explicitly **pre-prize**. It is not the 2017 winning build and not an enwik9 award. Below, totals, gain, dates and attribution follow that ranking; the linked evidence in each row concerns the component split. A dash means the official split was not independently established, not zero.

**5. starlit: change what the predictor sees next.** Alphabetical neighbors need not discuss similar things. STARLIT reorders articles by similarity so vocabulary and structures learned from one article remain in limited model memory when the next arrives. The compressor carries the order and dictionary, and the decoder restores the original order. Margaritov combined this with the adapted 2017 preprocessor and a reduced cmix: smaller PPM context models and a one-layer, 200-neuron LSTM (a recurrent neural predictor) traded capacity for time and RAM. The [author’s README](https://github.com/amargaritov/starlit) reports two machine-specific results: the official total matches the Intel run, not AMD’s 115,295,236. The 1.1% ranking gain is against the actual baseline; a temporary relaxation added 750,000 bytes to the comparison limit used for the claim. See also [Mahoney’s build and round-trip account](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1149).

**6. fast cmix: make the model affordable.** The intervening `cmix-hp` work enlarged a PPM model (which predicts from previously seen suffixes) and mapped it to disk to stay within the RAM limit. Kumar’s contribution targeted the CPU bottleneck: remove virtual calls in the hot path so the compiler can inline, store frequently used data together, reduce pointer chasing, and use faster hash containers. The [submission README](https://github.com/saurabhk/fast-cmix) explicitly distinguishes this from adding neurons in its first version. Its inherited `cmix-hp v3` table is *not* the winning measurement; use Kumar’s [Benchmark.md](https://github.com/saurabhk/fast-cmix/blob/HEAD/Benchmark.md) and the [committee’s award publication](https://groups.google.com/g/Hutter-Prize/c/ROHVm2_Xw0E).

**7. fx-cmix: predict Wikipedia syntax explicitly.** Orav replaced the PAQ8HP model inside fast-cmix with `fxcmv1`. Thirty main predictors divide up contexts; different states are relevant inside a table, list, template, quoted string or link. The model can switch contexts or turn a predictor off, and a match model exploits repeated sequences. This is not a claim that syntax alone produced the gain: the [author’s README](https://github.com/kaitz/fx-cmix) also reports a new dictionary and preprocessor changes. Its experiment gives **436,707 + 112,148,343 = 112,585,050**, not the official total. [Mahoney’s fx-cmix measurement](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1091) has a 112,142,259-byte archive; pairing it with the ranking would imply 436,063 bytes for S1, but that subtraction does not verify a matched official build.

**8. fx2-cmix: recover word information after dictionary coding.** Dictionary codes help compression but obscure the words a text predictor wants. The [authors’ README](https://github.com/kaitz/fx2-cmix) describes decoding a parallel text stream during prediction, stemming words and building different sentence and paragraph histories, in some histories dropping words such as articles and conjunctions. They also simplified mixers, made the Wikipedia transform single-pass, and derived a new article order offline using embeddings, dimensionality reduction and clustering. The order is the artifact used during compression, not an embedding-service call during decoding. The README’s split adds to the official total. [Mahoney’s prose](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1091) instead prints 441,468 for the compressor, five bytes too high for its own total. The ranking date is September; the README records the award on 8 October.

**9. cmix-lex: reorder structured metadata reversibly.** Marcouch integrated Orav’s stronger `fxcm_v26` model and article order; `payload_lex` rearranges a structured tail of the PHDA9-preprocessed stream. Compact side information, including permutation ranks, tells the decoder how to undo the change. The [author’s README](https://github.com/blahem/cmix-lex) gives **459,938 + 109,190,109 = 109,650,047** for the public files. [Mahoney’s report](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1091) explicitly identifies a different split as official: the table uses that verified run, not the public binary or its smaller internal payload. Joint credit follows the [award announcement](https://scanalyst.fourmilab.ch/t/hutter-prize-awards-largest-advance-in-20-years-on-its-superintelligence-benchmark/6468).

**10. cmix-obias: use the speed savings for a better probability estimate.** Freelan retained the cmix-lex transforms and used CPU optimizations to fund a 256-cell byte-level LSTM. A gated PPMd prior influences its output probabilities; an offline-trained correction model, `bitlstm32`, ships 23,002 bytes of half-precision weights. The [algorithm publication](https://huggingface.co/dfreelan/cmix-obias/blob/main/SUBMISSION.md) counts **459,989 + 23,002 = 482,991** for S1, plus **108,009,834** for S2: **108,492,825**. That is not the ranked 108,521,870. [Mahoney’s build table](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#1080) lists the bare binary and different AMD/Intel archive measurements, not a pair reconciling the award. The internal 107,726,514-byte payload is not S2 either. No unexplained residual is presented here as an official component.

**11. fx2-cmix-T: pay for offline learning when it saves more than it costs.** Ivanov branched from **fx2-cmix**, not from the chronologically preceding cmix-obias. He replaced its online LSTM with a six-million-parameter transformer trained to predict the next byte of each article, also receiving the PPMd prediction. Offline training took 26 hours on eight RTX 5090 GPUs; prize-time coding and decoding run on CPU. Quantized weights cost about 2.9 MB **in each executable**; low-precision arithmetic kept inference within budget. The model is specialized to enwik9: this result does not demonstrate the same advantage on unseen text. The [author’s technical publication](https://github.com/astOwOlfo/fx2-cmix-transformer-v1/blob/main/writeup.md) compares against then-pending cmix-lex; the official 7.46% instead uses cmix-obias. [Mahoney’s version table](https://mattmahoney.net/dc/text.html#0969) distinguishes the awarded July build from an August Intel bug fix scoring 100,420,830. The section anchor is shared with a later, ineligible fine-tune; read the subsection **fx2-cmix-transformer**, not **fx2-cmix-t-ft8**.

## Coverage limits

This is a census of **awards**, not of every submission, research paper, or Large Text Compression Benchmark record. Baselines, superseded claims, hardware-specific rebuilds and ineligible improvements are identified separately where they could be mistaken for winners. Verification here means comparing organizer records, published test reports and submitters’ technical documents; no binaries were executed and no compression runs were independently reproduced. All eleven official totals are established. Nine component pairs reconcile to the ranking; for fx-cmix and cmix-obias only the separate published measurements above were established. The old closed decoders also limit what can be said about their internals. Mechanisms are documented design changes, not controlled estimates of bytes saved by each change.
